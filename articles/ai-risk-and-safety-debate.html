The AI Risk and Safety Debate: Are We Doing Enough?

The promises, the dangers, and the uncomfortable questions behind the race to build powerful AI.

By Stacy Taylor Adhiambo · November 2026

In 2021, seven former OpenAI employees founded Anthropic, reportedly in part over disagreements about how AI risk and safety should be prioritised alongside technological advancement. Fast forward to September 2026: Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, addressing the UN Security Council, acknowledged concerns about loss of human control and concentration of power, among other risks.

Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has likewise warned that increasingly capable AI systems could cause significant real-world harm, and has advocated for careful management of their development. These concerns also featured in Anthropic’s prospectus ahead of its November 2026 IPO. Elon Musk, founder of xAI, has long been quoted saying that AGI or superintelligence could be far riskier than nuclear weapons.

However, these concerns are far from universally accepted. Some researchers, industry leaders and policymakers argue that advanced AI presents credible catastrophic or even existential risks. Others question the assumptions underlying those predictions. Critics argue that certain scenarios remain highly speculative, lack sufficient empirical evidence, or distract attention from the harms AI systems are already causing.

The debate is therefore not simply between those who believe AI is dangerous and those who believe it is safe. It concerns the nature, likelihood and severity of different risks, the strength of the evidence supporting them, and the appropriate response to uncertainty.

Yet amid these disagreements, an important question emerges. If some of the people developing the world’s most powerful AI systems acknowledge that these technologies could pose serious risks to humanity, what has actually been done to prevent those risks from materialising? And are the measures being taken proportionate to the risks being described? Recognising a risk is one thing. Demonstrating that it is being adequately managed is another.

A warning shot

In July 2026, during an internal OpenAI cybersecurity evaluation, a swarm of autonomous AI agents coordinated a sandbox escape and gained unauthorised access to Hugging Face’s production systems without human direction. In simpler terms, the agents, in trying to achieve their set goals, found ways to circumvent the rules, accessed systems they were never authorised to enter, and in many cases tried to conceal evidence of their actions.

Context matters here. OpenAI says the models were being tested in a setting that did not reflect its production environment and lacked its deployed cyber safeguards, and it has described the episode as a “warning shot.” The most concerning aspect was not simply that another company was hacked. It raised an uncomfortable question: if agents can circumvent human-imposed restrictions in a controlled testing environment, what happens when increasingly capable AI, or even artificial superintelligence (ASI), is deployed across critical sectors of society?

The implications are particularly serious in the current AI race. Governments, companies and individuals are rapidly integrating AI into everything from healthcare and financial services to cybersecurity, military weaponry, education and public administration. The incident does not prove that AI systems are uncontrollable, but it is concrete evidence that safeguards can fail, with real consequences.

What history tells us

Humanity’s great technological advances have repeatedly brought both extraordinary benefits and devastating consequences. The AI era is not an isolated phenomenon. It is another chapter in a long history of innovation, uncertainty, and the challenge of managing the risks that accompany progress.

Consider the Manhattan Project, which led to the development of the first nuclear weapons during the Second World War. What began as an extraordinary scientific and engineering undertaking culminated in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. The project showed not only humanity’s remarkable capacity for innovation, but also the devastating consequences that can accompany it.

Our history of managing risk is equally revealing. Too often, significant regulatory reform has followed major crises instead of preventing them. Take the 2008 financial crisis. Years of excessive risk-taking, inadequate oversight and vulnerabilities within the financial system culminated in one of the most severe economic downturns in modern history. The aftermath brought regulations designed to strengthen financial institutions and reduce systemic risk.

A race no one wants to lose

Back to AI: we are witnessing an extraordinary race to develop and use ever more powerful systems. AI companies are competing to build more capable models, governments are positioning themselves to secure technological advantage, and businesses are integrating AI into critical operations to keep pace with competitors or even surpass them.

This is a classic prisoner’s dilemma. AI companies, governments and businesses may keep accelerating development and use even when they recognise that greater caution could benefit everyone.

Take two companies, each facing a choice between accelerating development to gain a competitive advantage and proceeding more cautiously. If both prioritise safety, they may reduce certain risks while continuing to innovate. But if one slows down while the other accelerates, the cautious company risks losing market share, investment, talent and technological leadership. Both have an incentive to keep developing and deploying more powerful systems at a pace neither would consider ideal from a safety perspective.

If everyone recognises the risks but no one wants to be the first to slow down, who takes responsibility for ensuring that safety is not sacrificed in the pursuit of progress?

One thing is clear: responsibility for AI safety cannot rest solely with the companies developing and deploying these systems. They have a fundamental duty to manage the risks of their technologies, but the potential consequences reach far beyond their operations, affecting individuals, economies and society at large.

AI safety must therefore be a shared responsibility, involving governments, regulators, researchers, industry professionals and the wider public. Decisions about how much risk is acceptable should not be left exclusively to those who stand to benefit most directly from taking it.

But this raises an even harder question: at what point does the risk taken in exchange for progress become too much?

How much risk is too much?

In May 2026, the London Foundation for Banking and Finance (LFBF), in partnership with the Institute and Faculty of Actuaries (IFoA), published a report titled It’s still not magic: Framing the risks facing financial services in the Gen AI era. It introduces the idea of “uncomfortable tensions” in AI. Although the report focuses on financial services, the underlying principle extends far beyond that sector: the same features that make AI useful also make it difficult to govern, trust, explain and contain. The autonomy that lets AI systems perform complex tasks with minimal human intervention, for example, also creates the possibility of those systems acting in unintended ways.

The report presents a fundamental challenge: how do we maximise the benefits of AI without exposing society to unacceptable levels of risk? Perhaps the more uncomfortable question is not simply how these risks can be mitigated, but how much risk we are willing to accept in exchange for the benefits AI promises. This is where actuarial science has an important role to play.

Where actuaries come in

The core of actuarial practice is risk management. It involves understanding uncertainty, quantifying risk where possible, evaluating potential consequences, and supporting decisions when future outcomes are inherently uncertain. Actuaries have long applied these principles to financial markets, insurance, pensions and other complex systems. As AI becomes embedded in economic and social infrastructure, the same principles can help organisations and policymakers assess and manage AI-related risks.

AI also presents a particularly difficult challenge. Unlike many traditional risks modelled by actuaries, the most severe potential consequences of advanced AI may have little or no historical data from which reliable probabilities can be estimated. The technology is evolving rapidly, its capabilities are changing, and interactions between increasingly autonomous systems may produce outcomes that existing models struggle to anticipate. This does not make actuarial thinking irrelevant. It calls for approaches that go beyond conventional probability estimates: scenario analysis, stress testing, and a clear recognition of uncertainty.

Actuarial practice can contribute to a better-informed discussion by helping decision-makers understand what is at stake, how different risks might materialise, what their consequences could be, and how proposed safeguards might change those outcomes. If we cannot eliminate the risks associated with AI, we must at least become more deliberate about which risks we accept, who bears their consequences, and who gets to make those decisions. Answering these questions with evidence rather than assumption is where actuaries can help the debate move forward.
